Leaks you cannot see: why under-house trouble hides

Water that escapes in a crawlspace usually drops straight onto soil or a vapor barrier. Dirt soaks it up, so there is rarely a dramatic flood. Meanwhile the floor joists and subfloor above stay damp, and the problem only shows itself when wood softens or odors rise through floor registers.

Supply lines versus drain lines

A pressurized supply pipe leaks all the time, whether anyone is using water or not. A drain pipe only leaks when something upstream is draining, such as a flush, a shower, or the washing machine emptying. Noticing which pattern you have is one of the most useful things you can tell a plumber, because it narrows the search before anyone crawls in.

Older pipes, mixed materials, and loose hangers

Under many older homes you will find a mix of galvanized steel, copper, and newer plastic pipe joined over the years. Transitions between materials, joints that were never strapped, and lines that have sagged between hangers are typical failure points. Rodents and summer humidity do not help either.

Common signs of a leak you can notice from above the floor

  • A hiss or steady trickle under the house while the home is quiet and nothing is running.
  • The water meter dial still creeping after you have turned off every fixture indoors.
  • A musty, earthy odor that is strongest in one room or near a floor vent.
  • Vinyl or laminate lifting at the seams, or a spot near the toilet that flexes underfoot.
  • Sewage smell after flushing, which points toward a drain or waste line rather than supply.
  • More mosquitoes or standing water near the foundation vents than the weather would explain.
A quick meter test: close every tap, make sure the ice maker and washer are idle, and watch the small leak indicator on the meter for a few minutes. Movement with everything off strongly suggests a supply-side leak somewhere between the meter and the fixtures.

The first thirty minutes

You do not have to solve anything tonight. The goal is simply to stop more water from going where it should not, protect yourself, and gather details.

Shut the water where you can

Find the main shutoff. In many Albany homes it is at the meter box near the street, or on the line where it enters the house. A meter key or a sturdy pair of pliers usually turns the curb valve a quarter turn. If the leak only happens when a drain is used, you can leave the supply on and just stop using that toilet, tub, or sink.

Think about the electrical side

Crawlspaces often hold ductwork, wiring, and sometimes a pump or heater. If water is near anything electrical, stay out and leave it to a professional.

Write down what you noticed

Note when the sound started, which room is closest, and whether it changes when someone flushes. Photos of any wet flooring help too. These small notes save time once a plumber is on site.

Should you go under the house yourself?

For most people the honest answer is no. Crawlspaces can be tight, wet, and home to spiders, snakes, or exposed nails. If raw sewage is involved, the soil may be contaminated. A flashlight look through the access hatch is reasonable; a full crawl is not something you owe anyone.

If you do look from the hatch, check for a shiny wet trail on a pipe, a dripping joint, insulation sagging with water, or a dark patch of soil. Do not cut, clamp, or tape anything that is pressurized. A temporary fix that fails at midnight can be worse than the original drip.

Common questions about crawlspace leaks

How can I tell if the leak under my house is from a supply pipe or a drain?

Turn every fixture off and listen. If you still hear water or the meter keeps moving, it is likely a supply line. If the sound or dripping only appears after a flush, shower, or laundry cycle, a drain or waste pipe is the more likely source.

Is it safe to leave the water on overnight?

If a supply line is leaking, it is safer to close the main valve until a plumber can look. If only a drain leaks, you can usually keep the water on and avoid using the fixture that feeds it.

Do I need to be home when the plumber comes?

Someone should be there to open the house, show where the access hatch is, and answer questions. Let the dispatcher know if a neighbor or relative will be meeting the plumber instead.

My floor feels soft near the bathroom. Is that the plumbing?

It often is. A slow leak at a toilet flange or tub drain can soak the subfloor over months. A plumber can check the pipes below, and drying or replacing the damaged wood may follow.

Can you help with a smell under the house but no visible water?

Yes. A sewer or musty smell rising through vents is a common reason people call. Describe when it is strongest, and the plumber can inspect the drain and waste lines beneath the floor.
